Competitive Reality

A methodology for the strategic management of reputation.

Reputation cannot be bought. It is the consequence of the reality a brand creates.

Fama ex factis nascitur, non ex verbis.

Reputation is born of deeds, not of words.

Too often, communication becomes the starting point of a process that should have begun with a change in reality.

Over three decades of work – across mass media, international relations, public administration, the private sector, culture and the non-profit world – we have repeatedly observed the same phenomenon.

Where communication rested on genuine change beneath it, reputation eventually followed.

Where communication stood in for a substance that was missing, reputation sooner or later collapsed.

The Competitive Reality methodology was born as an answer to this experience.

It is not built on the question of how to communicate better. It begins much earlier. It asks whether a reality exists that communication can actually carry.

And whether that reality is competitive and attractive enough.

The answer to this question decides everything that follows.

If you believe that a brand, or a reputation, can be created with a logo, a slogan, a visual identity or an advertising campaign, this site is not meant for you.

All of that can create a brand's identity. It cannot create its reputation.

Reputation is not a graphic design. It is not a marketing product. It is not the outcome of a creative workshop. As a rule, it is the consequence of reality, of a brand's performance and of its competitiveness.

It arises from what a brand actually does, how it makes decisions, how it treats people, partners and its own promises – and how all of this has held up over time.

The Competitive Reality methodology therefore does not begin with communication. It begins by verifying reality. It asks a single question: is there anything at all that the world should believe about this or that brand?

If the answer is no, communication will most likely not help at all. If the answer is yes, communication is no longer the creator of reputation. It merely becomes its carrier.

The Competitive Reality methodology is designed above all for CEOs, senior management, public institutions, cities, regions, investors and other leaders.

It is designed for those who are no longer looking merely for better communication, but for a way to build, through the reality of their brand, greater credibility, a stronger position at home and abroad, and a lasting competitive advantage.

If you believe the word branding belongs to the domain of graphic design, you have most likely stumbled upon a website that says something entirely different.

Why we created this website

For more than thirty years we have watched the same phenomenon.

Companies, institutions, cities and even entire countries try to earn trust and respect through communication. They often even claim that communication about their dull and uninteresting substance, repeated often enough, will eventually produce a good reputation.

Some succeed. Many do not.

We gradually realized that the difference rarely lies in the quality of the communication. It lies in the quality of the reality that the communication is trying to convey.

Where a genuine reason to believe exists, communication accelerates trust. Where no such reason exists, it can almost never create it.

Over the years this idea was tested in journalism, in public institutions, with investors, in regions, in culture and in international projects. Gradually it grew into the methodology we now call Competitive Reality.

This website was not created to sell services. It was created to present this methodology for the first time as one coherent whole – and to present the projects from thirty years of work that confirm its credibility.

Method

Competitive Reality

Competitive Reality was also inspired by Simon Anholt's Competitive Identity approach, which I have developed over three decades into my own working methodology.

Simon Anholt showed that the reputation of countries is not the result of communication, but of their behaviour on the international stage. Competitive Reality extends this paradigm to companies, organizations and public institutions alike.

It does not work only with communication. It works with the whole reality of an organization – its identity, decisions, partnerships, products, services, spaces, events, symbolic acts, and the way this reality becomes legible to others.

Reputational architecture is built in one fixed order: first reality, then its transmission to an audience. Communication is the last step of this structure, not its foundation. Repeating dull communication about a brand's dull qualities simply does not work – unless you happen to be a yoghurt.

Serious brand management is about substance far more than about a logo, a website colour, or a name. It is about who you are, how you appear, how you want to appear, and why your reputation should change even without posting daily on social media about how great your brand is.

The seven phases of Competitive Reality

Building reputation on deeds, not words

7 phases of a strategic process from reality to communication and trust.

Foundation
01

Find out what is true

Start with reality, with facts, not with an analysis of communication. Most reputation problems do not originate in communication, but in the very substance of the brand.

02

Improve the substance

Remove the cause, not the symptoms. Communication cannot permanently substitute for a missing quality in reality.

03

Make reality legible

Reality alone is not enough. A complex reality must be made legible, not simpler. In other words, build a communication base able to carry news of the brand's changed behaviour.

Turning point
04

Design a symbolic act

Not a slogan. Not a campaign. Not an advertisement. Create one verifiable act that changes the way others perceive the brand.

05

Carry it out

The symbolic act must exist in reality. It is not enough to announce it, present it, or promise it. It must actually happen – only then can it carry trust.

Impact
06

Support the spread of its existence and its consequences

Only now does communication enter the picture. It is not the engine of change, but its distribution mechanism – carrying the consequences of real change to partners, investors, customers, media, employees and the public.

07

Track the change in reputation

Reputation is not a goal in itself. It is a measurable consequence. Track its impact on:

  • the trust and effectiveness of marketing communication,
  • business results and partnerships,
  • the ability to attract investors and talent,
  • the organization's long-term competitiveness.

Every new cycle of Competitive Reality begins again with reality, not with communication.

How this methodology differs

It is not PR. Nor public affairs. Nor journalism. Nor place branding. Nor classic brand strategy, as communication strategists call it.

Competitive Reality is not another communication discipline. It is a methodology that precedes communication. The difference from other professions lies not in technique, but in the order in which reality, trust and communication come into being.

PR

Works with the message about reality, not with reality itself – usually just passing information from the client to the mass media.

Classic PR starts with a message and retrofits facts to support it. Our methodology reverses the order: first verify and change reality; communication comes last – and speaks only of what actually happened and what is genuinely relevant.

Public affairs

Advises from the outside, does not carry the reality from within

Public affairs usually represents an organization's interests in the public space. Competitive Reality steps in even earlier – where the very quality of the reality that will one day need defending is decided. The difference is not in communication, but in the degree of responsibility for the decision.

Journalism

Verifies reality, but does not create it

A journalist preserves independence by not intervening in reality – only verifying it and reporting on it. Our methodology keeps that same discipline of verification, but combines it with the responsibility to also change reality, not merely to write about it.

Place branding

Designs an identity, but rarely measures capacity or actually changes reputation

Place branding usually begins by designing the desired identity of a place. Our approach to the reputation of a place diagnoses the measurable consequence of what the place can actually deliver – we are not interested in a design exercise that merely names an identity.

Brand strategy

Wants to manage reputation. It can only be earned.

Classic brand strategy is mostly understood as a tool for managing communication – channels, intensity and the frequency with which brand signals repeat. Our Competitive Reality stands on the opposite premise: reputation cannot be managed, it can only be earned through the reality an organization actually creates. The task of strategy is then not to manage how a brand appears from the outside, but to ensure it deserves what people say about it. Our approach is a deep dive into what a brand truly is, not merely into whether it has a planned communication strategy mistakenly called a branding strategy.

Proof of integrity, not of publicity

The greatest proof is not the report. It is the process that made it possible.

We do not create media coverage. We create the editorial conditions under which independent international journalism can decide that a story deserves to exist.

Vizovice is not a case study among others. It is the first practical application of the principle of transparent division of roles between Brand Consulting, SITA, Factstory and Agence France-Presse.

Vizovice · editorial independence · 2026

Transparent division of responsibilities, not personal ties.

The project connected strategic consulting, a national press agency, an international producer and a global news agency – a collaboration that naturally required clearly separated competencies. Editorial independence does not begin by denying the relationships between partners. It begins with openly acknowledging them and clearly separating competencies – precisely what made it possible to take a Central European story, the industrial heritage of Baťa, and the question of what replaces identity once a founding industry disappears, and bring it up to the standards of a global news agency.

CODES Brand House (concept and approach) → Brand Consulting (strategy and dramaturgy) → SITA (local journalistic expertise, fact-checking) → Factstory (video, press release and photo production to AFP standards) → Agence France-Presse (editorial processing and distribution on the international wire)

On the AFP wire in June 2026, reaching newsrooms in more than 150 countries. The closer a partner stood to the project's business or strategic interest, the more strictly its contributions were verified before entering the final material – the same principle on which the whole of Competitive Reality stands: first verify reality, only then let it communicate. It is precisely this discipline that is why the report remains a permanent part of the international AFP archive: even years from now, anyone in the world can look up and use the original video and materials again. Vizovice is the first practical proof that this discipline works even within a global news agency.

About Me

The person behind the methodology.

In 1996 he stands in a hall of seven hundred people. He watches an audience listening to an operatic recital, "Welcoming Spring with Martin Babjak," which he has just produced. None of the spectators has seen a single line of explanation about what is happening. No one has read a press release. The experience has already taken place before anyone asked communication to explain it. Martin Horváth is nineteen years old, and he does not yet know that he is observing a phenomenon to which he will devote thirty years of his life: perception, as a rule, does not simply follow a brand's communication. It follows reality.

He still speaks of this moment today as the point where his observation began. Not because he understood anything at the time. But because he remembered it as a question he would keep asking again, in ever more serious contexts, with ever higher stakes.

Before he became a manager or a consultant, he was a journalist. At seventeen he ran a school magazine, won a competition for young authors three times, and wrote for a regional daily. He studied journalism with a focus on print mass media and press agencies – institutions that do not create information, only distribute it faster than anyone else. He wrote his thesis on the PR tools of the state investment agency SARIO. That thesis unexpectedly redirected his entire career. He stopped writing about institutions. He started working for them.

That transition sounds like an ordinary career step. It is not. A journalist who becomes an insider carries with him a habit that cannot be unlearned: reading the gap between what an institution says about itself and what it actually is. From the outside, he has an eye for that gap but no power to change it. From the inside, he has the power, but risks losing the eye. Surviving a career exactly on this borderline is rare. It is perhaps the only place from which Competitive Reality could ever have emerged.

He learned his first great lesson on behalf of an entire country. At SARIO he led strategic marketing during the most intense period of foreign direct investment in Slovakia's history. During the Bush–Putin summit in 2005, the country earned coverage in Reuters, the Wall Street Journal, The Economist, Le Monde and the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung – and no one paid for it. A multinational company deciding where to place billions does not believe a campaign. It believes due diligence and what serious journalists independently write about a country. Today that sentence sounds like consultant's cliché. At the time it was a new and literal experience: a state cannot buy trust, it can only earn it.

Soon after came a series of moments in which the Slovak story had to stand before audiences with no reason to believe anything in advance. The national presentation at EXPO Zaragoza. An address to the London financial community at the Bank of England. The media side of Queen Elizabeth II's state visit, where there is virtually no room for error and preparation, not ideas, decides the outcome. A press briefing for four hundred and fifty journalists from around the world, where a complex national story had to be made intelligible to a skeptical audience all at once. Each of these experiences teaches something different. Together they teach one thing: persuasiveness can be rehearsed; credibility cannot.

Then came the lesson that shapes his work to this day. At the Slovak Tourist Board he prepared Slovakia's first major image campaign on CNN International. It was well made. It changed almost nothing. The reality beneath the image of the country could not yet carry what the campaign promised.

That led him to Simon Anholt, creator of the Competitive Identity concept, whose methodology gave a precise language to something he had until then reached only by instinct.

Communication is not a generator of trust. It is only its carrier – it transmits trust, but does not create it.

This claim can be written in ten seconds. Verifying it took another twenty years.

In 2010 he founds his own consulting practice, Branding Slovakia Consultancy – from this point on he tests the hypothesis not as an employee of institutions, but under his own name and on his own professional responsibility. The first test is Slovakia's entry into the Anholt-GfK Roper Nation Brands Index – the abstract framework of Competitive Identity turns into measurable data, not merely masterclass theory.

He tested it during the entry of the Swiss company Optotune into Slovakia, where he was responsible for governance affairs on a foreign direct investment of almost four million euros – an investment built on the trust of the academic and scientific community, not on marketing claims. He tested it on the Metrostav project, where he protected nearly sixty million euros following exactly this logic: the reputational transformation of the original TRINITY project into the functioning CityPark Ružinov was allowed to communicate externally only after the underlying technical reality had been fixed, not before. He tested it too at Robotec, a manufacturer based in Súča, where the Innovation Days concept was not an event but proof that a technology company's credibility comes from innovation, an expert community, R&D and content – part of the brief also included supporting the establishment of its robotics and automation R&D centre.

He tested it at the University Hospital of St. Martin, where, instead of a campaign ahead of a nearly three-hundred-million-euro investment, he proposed a single verifiable act – the blessing of the foundation stone by Pope Francis – and won the PROKOP Award, Slovakia's highest PR honour for it. He tested it with small entrepreneurs in the Trnava region, where he found no difference in substance, only in scale, between the trust of a state and the trust of a small business. He tested it at the OECD on the topic of Smart Cities, which, instead of declaring cities "smart," first measures what is actually true.

Gradually this work grows into more than a methodology – it becomes a platform. In 2020, together with several professionals, he founds Brand Slovensko 30 – not a one-off project, but a lasting professional ecosystem around the theme of Slovakia's reputation. With Simon Anholt he carries into it the same principles he first tested twenty years earlier as a participant in his masterclasses, or as the organizer of an event for the Slovak Tourist Board – now at the regional scale of Central Europe.

Six years later this ecosystem faces its first major practical test – not at a conference, but at the regional industrial cluster UNICO Innovation Hub in Vizovice, driven by the company UNICO Modular a.s., where reputation is no longer measured by media reach but by whether the region can hold its own in international competitiveness. The trust on which this test rests was not built by a single conference, but by long-term work – part of which included a report by Agence France-Presse that internationally verified the region's story ahead of the planned inaugural conference.

Alongside this whole experiment, a second, quieter one has been running from the start. In 2008, still as marketing director of the state agency, he collaborates with the legendary voice of Don LaFontaine on the English-language version of a promotional spot about Slovakia. It is the first moment he realizes that a voice alone can carry credibility before content even arrives. He has never stopped being fascinated by this discovery since – in dubbing, in theatre, in the question of what makes human presence distinguishable from the synthetic. It is the same question as in working with an institution's reputation, only in a different register: whether the unit of trust is one listener, or an entire country.

It is precisely this second line that finally lets him test the hypothesis where it is hardest to fake – in culture, where no one is paid to believe. Fairy Tales for Little Crayons, produced for CODES Brand House, is created as a charity project for visually impaired children – the methodology leaves the corporate world for the first time and proves that credibility can be built outside it too. From there grows the Dolby Atmos audio adaptation of The Jungle Book, a cinema project built entirely on spatial sound for blind and visually impaired audiences, and an immersive adaptation of The Phantom of the Opera, publicly endorsed by the family of Gaston Leroux, the novel's author.

The credibility of these projects does not come from communicating that they help or that they create something new. It comes from the fact that the help, or the innovation, actually works.

Shortly before that, in 2025, he becomes an advisor to the CEO of the Slovak Press Agency SITA and an occasional writer for its current-affairs productions. SITA has its own journalists, its own editorial standards and its own responsibility for accuracy – which makes it a natural partner for local journalistic fact-checking, held to exactly the same professional standards it applies to every other news item the agency publishes.

In 2026 the experiment reaches a place where it can be tested by the strictest possible judge – the world press agency AFP. A documentary about the industrial transformation of the Zlín Region, which he prepared and hosted under the direction of CODES Brand House, is produced according to a principle the partners agreed on after joint negotiations in Paris: each carries only the part of responsibility within its competence, and AFP handles the editorial processing and distribution of the entire material on its international wire. The report goes out on the Agence France-Presse wire to more than one hundred and fifty countries and remains a permanent part of its international archive. It is not the largest project of his career measured by budget. It is the largest in what it proves: the methodology he first noticed as the broken promises of a CNN campaign works exactly as he said it would, twenty years ago.

Today the methodology carries the name Competitive Reality. It was not born at a single table, and it was not invented by a consultant who sat down and decided to create a framework. It grew over thirty years in newsrooms, in ministries, with investors, in cities, in regions, in culture, in voice. It is the one case he can say with certainty he followed from the very beginning – because it was his own. It began in that same hall of seven hundred people, where he did not yet suspect that he was observing something he would one day name.

The second discipline

The human voice, and what makes something feel like the truth.

Alongside the strategy of reputation, another line has been developing for more than three decades.

Exploring what makes something feel like the truth even before language and words arrive. Voice, silence, rhythm, music, space and presence.

In an age of synthetic media, the authenticity of human presence expressed through voice is becoming a public good worth protecting.

It is the same question as working with reputation, only in a different register: whether the unit is a single listener, or an institution.

Milestones

Three decades of projects that shaped trust.

From the first newsroom in 1991 to the global AFP wire in 2026, one common thread runs through. This is not a list of job titles, whether in the private sector or in government ministries. It is the path along which the Competitive Reality approach gradually took shape.

The years are only reference points. The real story lies in the ideas that emerged during them.

01

First, I learned to observe.

Change does not arise in newsrooms. It arises in institutions and in public policy – and that is exactly where my career moved after my early years as a journalist.

1991–1996

First newsroom

At seventeen, as editor-in-chief, he runs the school magazine Starý blesk – three-time winner of the Štúrovo pero competition – and later writes for the regional papers Trnavský Hlas and Trnavské noviny. He occasionally contributes to national print media. An early experience that teaches him a story only works when it stands on something real.

1996–1997

MH agency

He founds and runs his own PR agency in Trnava – his first company, even before studying journalism. Its flagship project is the operatic recital "Welcoming Spring with Martin Babjak" for 700 people: the experience outpaces any communication about it, perception simply follows reality. A point he will describe, three decades later, as exactly where his observation began.

1996–2000

From journalism to institutions

Master's studies in journalism at Comenius University, focused on print media and press agencies. His thesis on the PR tools of SARIO unexpectedly redirects his entire career – from reporting on institutions to working for the institutions reporting is about.

1999

Internship in the Netherlands

An internship at The New Euroreporter gives him his first direct comparison of the Central European and Western European media worlds – how journalists and publishers handle sources, independence and the relationship of fact to narrative. The same comparison proves valuable more than three decades later, when he first collaborates with a team from Agence France-Presse.

2000

Verlagsgruppe Passau and BertelsmannSpringer

Production manager at MASMEDIA (Verlagsgruppe Passau), then PR manager at BertelsmannSpringer, where he runs the annual ARCH Award event on architecture and other cultural subjects. His last stop before the definitive turn towards the institutions he had until then only written about.

02

Why a country cannot buy trust with advertising

A multinational company does not believe a campaign. It believes due diligence, references and serious journalism. It believes the reputation of a country.

2001–2006

SARIO and the Bush–Putin summit

Director of strategic marketing at SARIO during Slovakia's most intense period of foreign direct investment inflows. During the Bush–Putin summit in 2005, the country earns unpaid coverage in Reuters, the Wall Street Journal, The Economist, Le Monde and the FAZ. Two years earlier, he leads the team that wins 3rd place in the "Best Marketing Campaign" category of the pan-European competition run by Strategic Direct Investor magazine.

2001–2006

Invest Report Slovakia

Editor-in-chief of the magazine SARIO distributed to embassies worldwide. Its design resembled The Economist – and although it was funded by a state agency, its content was written by journalists who never crossed the line between journalism and propaganda. His first encounter with the question of media independence, in the context of what today is often state propaganda.

2006–2009

First global campaign and meeting Anholt

As marketing director and acting general director of the Slovak Tourist Board, he leads, among other projects, the country's first above-the-line image campaign on CNN International – opening up, in Slovakia, the topic of reputation management and country branding.

In 2006 his story of inspiration by Simon Anholt, creator of the Competitive Identity methodology, begins; he completes the Nation Branding Masterclass in London and implements this concept as a manager at the Slovak Tourist Board again in Poprad (2008) – the first event of its kind in the entire V4 region – a turning point from communication towards reality.

Ongoing since 2006

Competitive Identity masterclasses

Co-organizes and moderates two events that bring Anholt's methodology to officials, institutions and companies across Central Europe. Teaching a framework to someone else forces you to understand it more honestly than merely applying it does.

2006–2009

International stages

The national presentation at EXPO Zaragoza is about event coordination; the address to the London financial community at the Bank of England is about public speaking – the first moments when the Slovak story must hold up before a sophisticated foreign audience, not just at home. Persuasiveness can be rehearsed, credibility cannot – a distinction that will be fully confirmed a few months later, at his meeting with Simon Anholt.

2008

State visit of Queen Elizabeth II

Leads the media side of Queen Elizabeth II's state visit to Bratislava – in an environment with virtually no room for error. It teaches him that at the highest level of representation, preparation and restraint decide the outcome, not ideas.

2008

SCIJ press briefing

An international press event – 450 journalists from around the world – teaches him the opposite discipline: how to make a complex national story intelligible to a large, skeptical and professionally distrustful audience all at once.

2009

Independent consultant

A ten-month bridge between the public sector and the private sector – independent advisory work on brand management for cities and regions, before joining Medissimo.

2010

Nation Brands Index

Enrols Slovakia in the Anholt-GfK Roper Nation Brands Index, the prestigious global study mapping how countries are perceived worldwide, and prepares a study for a state organization and its parent ministry. The abstract framework of Competitive Identity turns for the first time into measurable data, not just masterclass theory.

03

Working with reality, not around it

The task was never to improve the story. It was to make sure the story was true – and only then to make it legible.

2009–2010

Medissimo

Director of marketing, sales and CRM at Medissimo, one of the largest greenfield investments in Slovak private healthcare. His first top-management experience from inside a privately funded organization, rather than as an outside advisor.

2010–2011

Slovintegra

Group Brand Manager for the group that owned Medissimo. A reputation analysis with coverage in TREND magazine, a sponsorship project with the Slovak National Theatre's opera company, and a collaboration with the world tenor José Cura. His first experience that an industrial group's credibility can be built on high culture too, not only on its own business – a pattern he returns to more than a decade later with CODES Brand House's cultural projects.

2011–2016

Zuzana Grejtáková

Years of pro bono brand management for the Slovak oboist Zuzana Grejtáková – not as a publicist, but as producer of her public concerts and of The Oboe Plays Secret Garden project. An artist's personal brand is not built by a press release, but by the performance itself on stage and by innovative partnerships – including with the global brand NOKIA.

2010–2019

His own consulting practice

Through his own practice, Branding Slovakia Consultancy, he protects nearly €60 million on the Metrostav project – the reputational transformation of the original TRINITY project into the functioning CityPark Ružinov – by ensuring communication follows only after the underlying technical reality has been fixed, not the other way round. A subsequent project, "Live and Do Business," follows later. Within this practice he spends years working along the axis of a client's desired reputational architecture, combining symbolic acts, event management, PR, and both below-the-line and above-the-line communication.

2016–2018

Optotune

The entry of a Swiss company into Slovakia – responsible for governance affairs and communication for a foreign direct investment of nearly €4 million, built on real relationships with the academic and scientific community instead of marketing claims.

2012–2014

University lecturer, UCM Trnava

At the Faculty of Mass Media Communication he runs the BrandingSlovensko.com project and workshops for the Ministry of Foreign and European Affairs of the Slovak Republic on the Slovak brand – a public discussion that eventually draws in the President of the Slovak Republic. In 2015 he also co-founds the Marketing Masterclass in Trnava – the first platform where he confirms that teaching a methodology forces you to understand it better, a principle from which Brand Slovensko 30 grows five years later. Jeremy Hildreth, a globally renowned brand consultant, travels to the Trnava masterclass.

2014

Titusz Tóbisz at Bratislava Castle

Production of tenor Titusz Tóbisz's first charity concert at Bratislava Castle, for the client Deluvis. The young soloist's name reaches the public for the first time not only through media interviews, but through the fact that he sang in Bratislava and not in his home city of Košice.

2014

Superbrands Slovakia

Becomes a member of the Brand Council of Superbrands Slovakia – an independent panel that assesses brands not by marketing budget, but by real market credibility. He regards it as recognition from peers in the field.

2013–2014

Competitive Reality was not born at a single table. It grew over more than three decades in newsrooms, in institutions, in international projects, in consulting practice and in culture. This timeline is not its history. It lists some of the projects that stood at its origin.

How we work

We do not begin with communication. We begin with reality – understanding it, and asking whether it is competitive enough to deserve communication.

Reputation does not come from a better campaign. It comes from better decisions, stronger partnerships, and a reality that deserves communication. That is why we do not begin with a presentation or a marketing plan. We begin with the question of what needs to change in order for reputation to change too.

I.

Understanding

First, we listen.

Not to prepare a proposal.
To understand what the real problem is, what is merely its symptom, and whether entering the project makes sense at all.

II.

Diagnosing reality

We look for where reputation actually comes from.

We analyse identity, partnerships, environment, decision-making, symbolic acts, and what remains with people after contact with the subject. Communication is only one of these layers.

III.

Architecture of change

Only then do we design a solution.

Sometimes it is a strategic decision.
Sometimes a new partnership.
Sometimes a cultural project.
Sometimes a symbolic act.

Form is not the goal. It is only a tool.
The goal is to create a reality that naturally turns reputation into something attractive, into the result of a competitive reality.

If, after reading these lines, you feel we are not starting from the same place as most consulting and marketing firms, let's talk.

Not for a presentation.
For understanding.

Cost of collaboration

We have no price list.

An architect does not price a house by the number of bricks, and a surgeon does not price an operation by the number of incisions. In the same way, a strategic transformation of reputation cannot be priced by the number of meetings, presentations or hours worked.

The subject of this work is not merely documents, nor merely consultations. The subject of this work is strategic judgment. The ability to name a problem others cannot see. To design a solution that changes a brand's future decisions. To create an intellectual framework that will, over the long term, build or protect its value.

In this, Competitive Reality resembles the work of a top-tier lawyer or investment advisor. A well-drafted contract is not priced by the number of pages, but by the value it can protect or generate. And a sound strategic decision is not priced by hours worked, but by the future it makes possible.

That is why price is determined not merely by time spent with the client. It is determined by the strategic significance of the problem, the scope of the expected change, and the value that the right decision can bring or protect for the client.

How collaboration usually unfolds

Every brand is different. That is why there is no universal price list or universal workflow. In most projects, however, collaboration moves through four natural stages.

I.

Initial consultation

Collaboration usually begins with a roughly 30- to 45-minute meeting via ZOOM Workplace.

This consultation is free of charge. Its purpose is not to sell services, but to determine whether Competitive Reality is the right strategic approach for the brand in question. Equally important is verifying that we share a professional alignment in how we think about the problem.

If we conclude that we do not, we recommend a more suitable type of collaboration or a different expert.

II.

Strategic consultation

If further collaboration makes sense, the first working meeting follows, at a fixed price agreed in advance.

Over a few hours, we analyse the organization's situation, its ambitions, its reputational challenges and its strategic context. The goal at this stage is not yet to propose solutions, but to verify whether real potential exists to create a new competitive reality.

By the end of this phase, both sides already know whether it makes sense to continue. This diagnostic phase usually lasts 2 hours and is priced very competitively.

III.

Competitive Reality workshop

If the previous phase confirms the project's potential, we continue with an intensive workshop involving the organization's leadership and other key people. It usually lasts a full working day.

This is not a marketing brainstorm, nor a communication workshop. Together we analyse how the organization functions, internally and externally – its decision-making, relationships, opportunities and barriers. We look for the strategic changes that can naturally strengthen its credibility, competitiveness and reputation. And we try to identify the potential for a strong symbolic act that will bring the brand clear reputational momentum.

The summary of this meeting is recorded, since only a direct transcript can provide a solid foundation for a well-defined brief for the Reputational Transformation Masterplan.

IV.

Reputational Transformation Masterplan

The strategy itself is not created in a day. Depending on the project's scope, its preparation usually takes one to six months. During this time further consultations, analyses, hypothesis-testing and the gradual shaping of the final solution take place.

The result is not a marketing campaign, nor a communication plan. The result is the Reputational Transformation Masterplan – a strategic document that brings together the organization's current reality, the steps needed to change it, and the communication that will project this change outward, into one coherent system.

This is not a document filed away in a drawer after the presentation. It is a working framework for the decisions the brand will make in the years ahead.

Competitive Reality is not a consulting service sold by the hour. It is a strategic partnership designed for brands that want to consciously change their reality, so that their reputation changes too. Investing in building this strategy is an investment in the future value of the brand's reputation, which in turn shapes, among other things, the effectiveness of the client's sales and marketing.

The fee for the engagement varies according to the complexity of the subject and the brand's standing at the time our relationship begins.
